Every air conditioning unit sold in the UK carries an energy label, the same style as the ones on fridges and washing machines, and it can be genuinely useful once you know what the letters actually stand for.

## SEER: cooling efficiency

SEER stands for Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio. Rather than measuring efficiency at one fixed test temperature, it is calculated across a representative range of outdoor conditions over a cooling season, which gives a more realistic picture of how a system performs in normal use rather than in a lab. In simple terms, a higher SEER means more cooling delivered for the same amount of electricity.

## SCOP: heating efficiency

SCOP, the Seasonal Coefficient of Performance, is the equivalent measure for heating. If you are considering a reverse-cycle system that will also warm the room in the cooler months, this is the number that tells you how efficiently it does that side of the job. It matters just as much as SEER if you plan to use the system year-round rather than for cooling alone.

## Where the label comes from

This is not marketing shorthand, the energy labelling of air conditioners is set out in law. The requirements originate in EU energy labelling regulations that continue to apply in the UK, and the [full regulation is published on legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2011/626/contents) for anyone who wants the detail behind the label rather than a summary of it.

## Why the letter grade is not the whole story

The A+++ to G scale on the label is a useful shorthand, but two units with the same letter can still have meaningfully different SEER and SCOP figures underneath it, since the bands cover a range. When we are comparing systems for a quote, we look at the actual numbers rather than the letter alone, particularly for a system that will be running through both summer and winter.

## Why efficiency matters beyond the running cost

A more efficient system costs less to run, but it also tends to mean better-engineered components, quieter operation, and a compressor that is not working as hard to hit the same result. Efficiency and comfort tend to move together, not against each other.
