It is tempting to assume a bigger unit will simply cool a room faster and better. In practice, an oversized system is one of the more common reasons people end up disappointed with an installation, and it has nothing to do with the brand.

## Why oversizing is a real problem, not a safety margin

A unit that is too large for the room cools it quickly and then switches off, before it has had time to properly dehumidify the air. The room reaches temperature but still feels clammy, and the system short-cycles, switching on and off repeatedly rather than running efficiently at a steady state. That is harder on the equipment and does not actually deliver better comfort.

An undersized system has the opposite problem: it runs constantly, struggling to reach the target temperature at all, particularly on the hottest days when you need it most.

Oversizing is common enough across the industry that [CIBSE, the UK's professional body for building services engineers, has written about it directly](https://www.cibsejournal.com/technical/sizing-the-opportunity-using-software-tools-to-reduce-oversizing-of-hvac/), including how easy it is for a rough calculation to overestimate the load a system actually needs to handle.

## What actually goes into sizing a system

A proper sizing calculation is not a guess based on square footage. It accounts for:

- **Room dimensions** and ceiling height, which set the base volume of air being cooled
- **Glazing** — how much glass, which direction it faces, and whether it gets direct sun
- **Insulation and construction** — a well-insulated modern room holds a set temperature more easily than an older one with solid walls
- **Heat sources in the room** — electronics, occupancy, and anything else adding heat load
- **Adjoining spaces** — whether the room is isolated or open to the rest of the house

This is standard practice across the industry, and it is why a proper survey takes account of the actual room rather than a size in square metres pulled from a floor plan.

## Ducted and multi-room systems

The same principle applies at a larger scale. For a multi-split or ducted system covering several rooms, the outdoor unit needs to be matched to the combined load of everywhere it serves, with some allowance for how many rooms are realistically running at once. Get that wrong and you either pay for capacity nobody uses, or end up with a system straining on the one day everyone is home and it is thirty degrees outside.
